Languages & naming¶

The languages of the Grand Duchy, how Lithuanian names are built, and the saga’s invented true names and incantation grammar.

Labels. [Historical record] is attested in the sources or standard scholarship; [The saga’s invention] was made for this series.

In 1382 nobody in Lithuania could write a sentence in Lithuanian. The Grand Duke’s letters went out in Latin or Ruthenian, the Order counted in German, the priests prayed in Latin or Church Slavonic, and the old gods were spoken to in a language that had never been written down. That last fact is the saga’s opening. The Senovės Kalba is powerful partly because it lives only in mouths.

Interpreters. The Order employed professional interpreters, called Tolken in Order documents, many of them Prussians or Lithuanian converts. An interpreter in ch. 18–19 is both accurate and useful: the Grand Master hears Vytautas’s words after a stranger has chosen them.

Writing in Book I. The earliest surviving Lithuanian text is a handful of prayers handwritten into a book printed in Strasbourg in 1503. The first printed Lithuanian book is Mažvydas’s catechism (Königsberg, 1547). So a written Lithuanian word, letter or inscription in 1382–87 is an anachronism. If the plot needs a written message between Lithuanians, it is in Ruthenian or Latin, or it is not written at all: a token, a knotted cord, an amber piece, a krivulė (the krivis’s crooked staff, carried as a summons).

Christian words are borrowed [Historical record]. Lithuanian took its Christian vocabulary from Ruthenian and Polish: krikštas (baptism), bažnyčia (church), kryžius (cross), griekas (sin), dūšia (soul), knyga (book). A pagan in 1382 hearing these words hears foreign words, which is a strong effect to use in POV. The native words for the sacred are older: dievas (god, cognate with Latin deus), šventas (holy), siela and vėlė (soul), auka (offering), alkas (grove). Even kunigaikštis (duke) is built on kunigas “lord”, an early loan from Germanic *kuningaz “king”.

A correction to the brief’s divisions. Algirdas divides Al-girdas, not Algi-rdas, because -girdas recurs as an element (Mantigirdas, Visgirdas). Gediminas is usually divided Gedi-minas, with a linking vowel.

Žygimantas is not native. It is the Lithuanian form of German Sigismund, folk-reshaped to look like žygis “campaign” + -mantas. Žygimantas Kęstutaitis’s name is therefore a sign of his Christian upbringing; his pagan name, if he had one, is unknown.

2.2 Short and diminutive names [Historical record]¶

Alongside the compounds there are short names, often one element plus a suffix: -utis/-utė (Jaunutis “little young one”, Birutė, Danutė), -enis (Vytenis, Lengvenis), -ys, -elis/-ėlė. Many feminine names of the dynasty are of this kind. Medieval women’s names are poorly recorded. Most noblewomen appear in the sources only under their baptismal names (Ona, Elena, Marija, Sofija, Julijona, Eufemija, Kotryna).

2.3 Patronymics and family names [Historical record]¶

  • No surnames in the 14th century. A person is X, son/daughter of Y, or X of [place].

  • Lithuanian patronymic suffixes: -aitis (son: Žygimantas Kęstutaitis, Andrius Algirdaitis); -aitė (daughter: Danutė Kęstutaitė); -ėnas (son or descendant, especially in the east: compare sūnėnas “nephew”, “brother’s son”). The suffix -áitis is always stressed.

  • Ruthenian patronymics: -ovich/-evich (son), -ovna/-evna (daughter). Vytautas Kęstutaitis is Vitovt Keistutyevich in Ruthenian, and Jogaila Algirdaitis is Jagailo Olgerdovich.

  • Dynastic plurals (Gediminaičiai, Kęstutaičiai, Algirdaičiai, Jogailaičiai) are modern historians’ terms. They are fine in narration and anachronistic in dialogue.

  • Married names in -ienė (Kęstutienė) belong to the later surname system. Do not use them in Book I. Say Kęstučio žmona “Kęstutis’s wife”, or let the title carry it (kunigaikštienė “duchess”).

4. The Senovės Kalba as Old Lithuanian¶

4.1 What it is¶

[The saga’s invention] In the world, the Senovės Kalba (“the Ancient Speech”) is Lithuanian in its oldest register, the words the elements were first addressed in. It was handed down by mouth from krivis to žynys and from mother to daughter, and never written. Mortals ask in it, and the elements answer only to it.

[Historical record] In fact, the five incantations are standard modern Lithuanian. Real Old Lithuanian, as the 16th–17th-century texts preserve it, had features that modern Lithuanian has lost or reduced. The saga can draw on them for flavour:

  • Athematic verbs in -mi: esmi “I am”, eimi “I go”, duomi “I give” (modern esu, einu, duodu).

  • The permissive with te-: teesie “let it be”, teateinie “let it come”, tebūnie “let it be” (Daukša’s Lord’s Prayer, 1595).

  • A living dual number: mudu “we two”, judu “you two”, dvi žvaigždi “two stars”.

These are attested forms. Inventing new archaic forms (for example a permissive *tešvytie “let it shine”) should be checked with a historical linguist first. A wrong “archaism” will jar a Lithuanian reader more than plain modern Lithuanian.

The grammar, and why it matters to the magic:

  1. The element comes first, in the nominative. Naming the power is the address, so the element is spoken first. Never reorder the words.

  2. The pronoun comes second, in the dative. tau means “for you” (cast for another; the caster pays) and man means “for me” (cast on oneself; the caster pays, but less). These are unstressed clitics.

  3. The verb comes last, in the 3rd-person future. The appendices gloss it as a wish (“Tegul žvaigždės tau švytėtų”, “May the stars shine”). Grammatically it is a statement about the future: “the stars will shine.” This is the saga’s best hidden rule. A working is a prophecy the caster asks the element to make true. Under the Law of Truth a false statement fails, so a caster cannot ask for what they do not truly intend (Magic rules §3.2). The Lithuanian 3rd person is the same in singular and plural, which is why žvaigždės (plural) and ugnis (singular) take the same form of verb.

  4. The verb is the element’s own natural act: stars shine, fire burns, earth holds, wind carries.

  5. Every line ends on a stressed syllable (švy-TĖS, DEGS, TEKS, lai-KYS, NEŠ). New lines should keep this hard, falling-shut cadence.

The water line’s double sense. Teks is the future of tèkti “to fall to one’s share; to have to”, not of tekėti “to flow” (whose future is tekės). Read strictly, Vanduo tau teks means “water shall be your portion”. That is a fitting oath for the element that heals by taking the patient’s wound onto the healer (Magic rules §4.1). Keep the line as it is, and let a learned character (the krivis, Birutė) notice the double meaning once. If the author ever wants the plain sense “water will flow for you”, the line would be Vanduo tau tekės.

The Five-Voice invocation (Penkių Balsų Šauksmas) speaks the five lines in the order Stars, Fire, Water, Earth, Wind, from the sky down to the air around the caster.

5. The true name: slaptas vardas [The saga’s invention]¶

Required by the Senovės Sutartis.

  1. What it is. At birth, before any public name is given, the mother whispers a secret name into the child’s ear. Only she and the elements know it. The public name (Vytautas, Eglė) is given later, at the naming feast.

  2. How it is given. In the first hour, over the five media if she can manage it: a candle (fire), a bowl of water, a pinch of earth, an open window (wind), and starlight or amber (stars). Most mothers manage two or three. The elements present are the elements that will “know” the child.

  3. What it sounds like. A single old word, never a two-part princely name. Often it is something the mother saw or felt at the birth (a bird, a weather, a tree), or a nature word with an old diminutive. The point is that it sounds nothing like a duke’s name. Do not invent Vytautas’s true name in the docs. Magic_rules §6 has Birutė speak it in ch. 3 and leaves it as a mystery for later. The reader should hear only that it was a word older than “Vytautas” (author’s decision).

  4. Who may speak it. Only the mother; the bearer, if they ever learn it; a binder in a Senovės Sutartis; and an Iron Wolf candidate, who whispers his own to a sacred flame at his making. Speaking another’s true name to anyone else is the gravest breach of trust a Lithuanian of the saga can commit.

    • The skin-name. Distinct from the true name, an Iron Wolf also bears a kailio vardas (“skin-name”). It is a public nickname, a dead enemy’s name, or an object. Kęstutis’s armour-bearer Jonas wears the baptismal name of an Order sergeant he killed. The skin-name hides nothing and binds nothing.

  5. What it does. It addresses the person beneath every title, baptism and mask. This is why baptism cannot overwrite it. Vytautas is Wigand and then Aleksandras to the Church, but his true name is unchanged, and this is why the amber answers to his truth, not his creed (Magic rules §5.4). Under Magic rules §10, someone who knows your true name better than you do is beyond your workings.

  6. Hooks. The Shadow-Seller collects true names. A mother who dies in childbirth takes the name with her, and that child is unbindable, which is a blessing and a curse. When the Root-Mother takes Eglė, she names her anew (Pelkių Eglė), which is what “taken” means.
