Amber Craft

Lore of the Craft: Gintarystė, amber as a medium

lore

“Amber is the sun’s grief set hard. Hold it and it will tell you what it has seen. Lie to it and it will tell you that too.”

[The saga’s invention] Palanga amber-keepers’ lesson, first line.

Saulė’s grief

In the saga’s cosmology Saulė, the Sun, weeps, and every tear is amber. That is why amber stores memory and carries workings (Cosmology, §3.1; Gintarystė). It is the only medium all five elements accept.

[Historical record] Baltic amber has been traded since antiquity (Tacitus, Germania 45, has the Aestii gathering it). The folk record ties it to the sea goddess Jūratė’s shattered palace. The Teutonic Order controlled the Prussian amber trade and punished private gathering harshly. That amber remembers, and that it is Saulė’s tears, is [The saga’s invention] overlay.

How amber is charged

Kind What it is Who can use it
Raw Any amber; carries any one working A caster
Charged A working breathed into it, released later by the bearer with the right word Even an ungifted bearer
Anchored Charged with the caster’s blood; links caster and bearer across any distance One anchor per caster; a second breaks the first
Memory A last memory taken from one who dies holding it Seers, who may be shown it

Palanga’s law: amber taken from the shore after a storm without an offering “goes cold for its whole life”. Hanseatic merchants carry boxes of dead amber and never know. The Order’s Bernsteinmeister burn charged pieces in consecrated furnaces, a secret doctrine of the Iron Saint.

The pebble as barometer of truth

The pebble answers to the truth of the bearer, not to his faith. The table below is the barometer set out in Magic rules, §5.4, extended by what later books show.

Chapter State Why
Book I ch. 3 Warm, pulsing Freely given in love
Book I ch. 8-9 Faint light in the dark He is true to his grief
Book I ch. 19 Dead, cold stone Baptised as a lie: he neither believes nor means to keep the oath
Book I ch. 20 Burning, then cold again Žemyna’s price for the burned village, a blister under the pouch
Book I ch. 23 Hot He chooses truly: betrays the Order to save Vilnius
Book I ch. 29 Glowing His 1387 baptism is honest about its dishonesty: for his people, eyes open
Book III ch. 17 Light through the leather An honest oath to Perkūnas before Grunwald
Book IV ch. 22 Burning like a hot iron He refuses Henė’s crown; a second, smaller scar opens beside the first
Book IV ch. 28 Warm, level, silent He believes both things at once; nothing to hide

Two cautions are in the text. The pebble also dies at the Order’s chapel because of consecrated stone, and Book I ch. 19 lets the reader hold both explanations: stone and lie. And while Vytautas carries the Order’s sword, he cannot use the amber; once he throws it into the burning tower (Book I ch. 24), “the spring that he could not summon an hour before began to flow again”.

Birutė’s Covenant

The Sutartis of Book I ch. 3 binds amber to a person: the binder’s blood on a charged piece, the bound person’s true name and a promise both ways (Birutė’s Senovės Sutartis). Through the anchor Birutė reaches Krėva from Palanga, because distance is free; the price is paid elsewhere, in her voice and sight. After Book I ch. 27 the pebble stays “warm, but no longer alive, like a hand that has not yet withdrawn”. At Vytautas’s death it cracks (Book IV ch. 29). See The Price and Oaths and pacts.

The silver wolf’s role

The silver wolf is not amber and carries no spell. It is an identity token carved with the Iron Wolves’ sign, so every vilkatas who sees it knows the bearer (Silver wolf). It belongs to the brotherhood, not the holder: it cannot be given away by one man, only passed on hand to hand, “bread for bread”. Its custody runs Kęstutis, Vytautas, Pilkoji, the Flame-Keeper.

Beside the pebble, the pair divide the labour. The amber answers to truth and links a mother to her son. The silver says who you are to the pack. Neither works as a weapon.

Nothing links them: no source says the silver wolf and the amber interact. This page sets them side by side as parallel carried objects and does not assert any link between them.

After the kneeling

Birutė’s last anchor on land is the amber kneeling woman at Palanga (the kneeling woman). Whoever moves her dies at sea. In Book V ch. 5 the narrating voice is an amber bead itself, the one the Krivis cast into the flame at the oath of 1345, which “did not fall” but rose and hung in the fire, and remembers.
