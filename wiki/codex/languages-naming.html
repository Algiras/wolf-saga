Languages & naming

Source: world_rules/languages_and_naming.md

Canonical reference for the languages spoken in the saga’s world, how its names are built, and the saga’s invented “true names” and incantation grammar. Pronunciation of the names below is in pronunciation_guide.md (and pronunciation_lexicon.json). Spelling decisions follow canon_decisions.md D4 and D8. The magic mechanics that use true names are in magic_rules.md §3 and §6.

Labels: [Record] is attested in historical or linguistic sources. [Saga invention] was made for this series. (verify) is to be checked before it goes into chapter text.

In 1382 nobody in Lithuania could write a sentence in Lithuanian. The Grand Duke’s letters went out in Latin or Ruthenian, the Order counted in German, the priests prayed in Latin or Church Slavonic, and the old gods were spoken to in a language that had never been written down. That last fact is the saga’s opening. The Senovės Kalba is powerful partly because it lives only in mouths.

1. The languages of the Grand Duchy in the 1380s [Record]

Interpreters. The Order employed professional interpreters, called Tolken in Order documents (verify term), many of them Prussians or Lithuanian converts. An interpreter in ch. 18–19 is both accurate and useful: the Grand Master hears Vytautas’s words after a stranger has chosen them.

Writing in Book I. The earliest surviving Lithuanian text is a handful of prayers handwritten into a book printed in Strasbourg in 1503 (date of the handwriting c. 1503–25; verify). The first printed Lithuanian book is Mažvydas’s catechism (Königsberg, 1547). So a written Lithuanian word, letter or inscription in 1382–87 is an anachronism. If the plot needs a written message between Lithuanians, it is in Ruthenian or Latin, or it is not written at all: a token, a knotted cord, an amber piece, a krivulė (the krivis’s crooked staff, carried as a summons).

Christian words are borrowed [Record]. Lithuanian took its Christian vocabulary from Ruthenian and Polish: krikštas (baptism), bažnyčia (church), kryžius (cross), griekas (sin), dūšia (soul), knyga (book). A pagan in 1382 hearing these words hears foreign words, which is a strong effect to use in POV. The native words for the sacred are older: dievas (god, cognate with Latin deus), šventas (holy), siela and vėlė (soul), auka (offering), alkas (grove). Even kunigaikštis (duke) is built on kunigas “lord”, an early loan from Germanic *kuningaz “king”.

2. How Lithuanian names are built

2.1 Two-part (dithematic) names [Record]

Old Lithuanian princely and boyar names are compounds of two elements, like Germanic Wil-helm or Slavic Vladi-mir. The same elements recur in many names, which is how scholars identify them. The meanings below are the ones commonly proposed in Lithuanian onomastics. Most are probable, few are certain, and the confidence column is honest.

jo- joti “to ride” Jo-gaila (“strong rider” is the usual gloss) Plausible

A correction to the brief’s divisions. Algirdas divides Al-girdas, not Algi-rdas, because -girdas recurs as an element (Mantigirdas, Visgirdas). Gediminas is usually divided Gedi-minas, with a linking vowel.

Žygimantas is not native. It is the Lithuanian form of German Sigismund, folk-reshaped to look like žygis “campaign” + -mantas. Žygimantas Kęstutaitis’s name is therefore a sign of his Christian upbringing; his pagan name, if he had one, is unknown (verify).

2.2 Short and diminutive names [Record]

Alongside the compounds there are short names, often one element plus a suffix: -utis/-utė (Jaunutis “little young one”, Birutė, Danutė), -enis (Vytenis, Lengvenis), -ys, -elis/-ėlė. Many feminine names of the dynasty are of this kind. Medieval women’s names are poorly recorded. Most noblewomen appear in the sources only under their baptismal names (Ona, Elena, Marija, Sofija, Julijona, Eufemija, Kotryna).

2.3 Patronymics and family names [Record]

  • No surnames in the 14th century. A person is X, son/daughter of Y, or X of [place].
  • Lithuanian patronymic suffixes: -aitis (son: Žygimantas Kęstutaitis, Andrius Algirdaitis); -aitė (daughter: Danutė Kęstutaitė); -ėnas (son or descendant, especially in the east: compare sūnėnas “nephew”, “brother’s son”). The suffix -áitis is always stressed.
  • Ruthenian patronymics: -ovich/-evich (son), -ovna/-evna (daughter). Vytautas Kęstutaitis is Vitovt Keistutyevich in Ruthenian, and Jogaila Algirdaitis is Jagailo Olgerdovich (verify exact spellings).
  • Dynastic plurals (Gediminaičiai, Kęstutaičiai, Algirdaičiai, Jogailaičiai) are modern historians’ terms. They are fine in narration and anachronistic in dialogue.
  • Married names in -ienė (Kęstutienė) belong to the later surname system. Do not use them in Book I. Say Kęstučio žmona “Kęstutis’s wife”, or let the title carry it (kunigaikštienė “duchess”).

3. Spelling rules for the project

  1. Always full diacritics (Kęstutis, not Kestutis). In English docs as well as Lithuanian text: the marks change the sounds.
  2. D4 names: Vytautas, Jogaila, Kęstutis, Ona, Skirgaila, Birutė, Švitrigaila, Jadvyga (Jadwiga in Polish context), Henė (Henne in English docs). Vyti is a vocative used only by Birutė and Kęstutis. Vytis is the coat of arms.
  3. One spelling per castle: Krėva (not Kreva, Krewa or Krewo); Astravas (Ostrów); Ašmena.
  4. D8 terms: Aukso Rijūnas (not Auksinis Smauglys), Šaknų Motina (not a synonym for Žemyna), Dykra.
  5. Latin epithets should be grammatical: Sanctus Ferrum mixed genders (ferrum is neuter), so it is corrected to Ferrum Sanctum (“Holy Iron”); Sanctus Ferreus (“the Iron Saint”) is the other grammatical option. Mercator-Rex is fine. (D8 now records Ferrum Sanctum.)
  6. Persona files’ “True Name” field should be renamed “Historical name”, so that it does not collide with the saga’s true name (§5).

4. The Senovės Kalba as Old Lithuanian

4.1 What it is

[Saga invention] In the world, the Senovės Kalba (“the Ancient Speech”) is Lithuanian in its oldest register, the words the elements were first addressed in. It was handed down by mouth from krivis to žynys and from mother to daughter, and never written. Mortals ask in it, and the elements answer only to it.

[Record] In fact, the five incantations are standard modern Lithuanian. Real Old Lithuanian, as the 16th–17th-century texts preserve it, had features that modern Lithuanian has lost or reduced. The saga can draw on them for flavour:

  • Athematic verbs in -mi: esmi “I am”, eimi “I go”, duomi “I give” (modern esu, einu, duodu).
  • The permissive with te-: teesie “let it be”, teateinie “let it come”, tebūnie “let it be” (Daukša’s Lord’s Prayer, 1595).
  • A living dual number: mudu “we two”, judu “you two”, dvi žvaigždi “two stars”.

These are attested forms. Inventing new archaic forms (for example a permissive *tešvytie “let it shine”) should be checked with a historical linguist first (verify). A wrong “archaism” will jar a Lithuanian reader more than plain modern Lithuanian.

4.2 The formula (from magic_rules.md §3.1)

[Element] [tau / man] [verb].

Element Line Literal sense
Stars (Praurimė) Žvaigždės tau švytės “The stars will shine for you”
Fire (Gabija) Ugnis tau degs “Fire will burn for you”
Water (Bangpūtys) Vanduo tau teks “Water will fall to your lot” (see below)
Earth (Žemyna) Žemė tau laikys “Earth will hold (it) for you”
Wind (Vėjopatis) Vėjas tau neš “Wind will carry (it) for you”

The grammar, and why it matters to the magic:

  1. The element comes first, in the nominative. Naming the power is the address, so the element is spoken first. Never reorder the words.
  2. The pronoun comes second, in the dative. tau means “for you” (cast for another; the caster pays) and man means “for me” (cast on oneself; the caster pays, but less). These are unstressed clitics. (A possible extension, not canon: jam/jai “for him/her”, used only through a charged amber anchor for someone absent; mums “for us” for a joint working.)
  3. The verb comes last, in the 3rd-person future. The appendices gloss it as a wish (“Tegul žvaigždės tau švytėtų”, “May the stars shine”). Grammatically it is a statement about the future: “the stars will shine.” This is the saga’s best hidden rule. A working is a prophecy the caster asks the element to make true. Under the Law of Truth a false statement fails, so a caster cannot ask for what they do not truly intend (magic_rules §3.2). The Lithuanian 3rd person is the same in singular and plural, which is why žvaigždės (plural) and ugnis (singular) take the same form of verb.
  4. The verb is the element’s own natural act: stars shine, fire burns, earth holds, wind carries.
  5. Every line ends on a stressed syllable (švy-TĖS, DEGS, TEKS, lai-KYS, NEŠ). New lines should keep this hard, falling-shut cadence.

The water line’s double sense. Teks is the future of tèkti “to fall to one’s share; to have to”, not of tekėti “to flow” (whose future is tekės). Read strictly, Vanduo tau teks means “water shall be your portion”. That is a fitting oath for the element that heals by taking the patient’s wound onto the healer (magic_rules §4.1). Keep the line as it is, and let a learned character (the krivis, Birutė) notice the double meaning once. If the author ever wants the plain sense “water will flow for you”, the line would be Vanduo tau tekės.

The Five-Voice invocation (Penkių Balsų Šauksmas) speaks the five lines in the order Stars, Fire, Water, Earth, Wind, from the sky down to the air around the caster.

4.3 Writing new workings [Saga invention]

  • Keep the three-word shape. A fourth word may be a direct object placed before the verb (Vėjas tau žodį neš “Wind will carry the word for you”) (proposal; check the rhythm aloud).
  • Candidate verbs (3rd-person future). These are suggestions; check stress before use:
Element Verbs
Stars švytės “will shine”, rodys “will show”
Fire degs “will burn”, šildys “will warm”, saugos “will guard”
Water teks “will fall to one’s lot”, plaus “will wash”
Earth laikys “will hold”, slėps “will hide”
Wind neš “will carry”, pūs “will blow”
  • No Christian or Slavic loanwords in the Kalba (dūšia, griekas, kryžius). The elements do not know them, which is a rule worth a scene.
  • No modern or abstract coinages. Regėjimo Aiška (the Stars boon) uses aiška, which is not a standard noun. It works as an archaic-sounding name for the boon, but avoid it inside an incantation.
  • Vows in Kalba scenes use plain, short sentences: Jo skola — mano skola (“His debt is my debt”); Kol aš gyvas, paskutinė ugnis neužges (“While I live, the last fire will not go out”).

5. The true name: slaptas vardas [Saga invention]

Required by the Senovės Sutartis (magic_rules.md §6, requirement 3).

  1. What it is. At birth, before any public name is given, the mother whispers a secret name into the child’s ear. Only she and the elements know it. The public name (Vytautas, Eglė) is given later, at the naming feast.
  2. How it is given. In the first hour, over the five media if she can manage it: a candle (fire), a bowl of water, a pinch of earth, an open window (wind), and starlight or amber (stars). Most mothers manage two or three. The elements present are the elements that will “know” the child.
  3. What it sounds like. A single old word, never a two-part princely name. Often it is something the mother saw or felt at the birth (a bird, a weather, a tree), or a nature word with an old diminutive. The point is that it sounds nothing like a duke’s name. Do not invent Vytautas’s true name in the docs. Magic_rules §6 has Birutė speak it in ch. 3 and leaves it as a mystery for later. The reader should hear only that it was a word older than “Vytautas” (author’s decision).
  4. Who may speak it. Only the mother; the bearer, if they ever learn it; a binder in a Senovės Sutartis; and an Iron Wolf candidate, who whispers his own to a sacred flame at his making (iron_wolves.md: “the name stays in the fire”). Speaking another’s true name to anyone else is the gravest breach of trust a Lithuanian of the saga can commit.
    • The skin-name. Distinct from the true name, an Iron Wolf also bears a kailio vardas (“skin-name”). It is a public nickname, a dead enemy’s name, or an object. Kęstutis’s armour-bearer Jonas wears the baptismal name of an Order sergeant he killed (persona/Jonas/description.md). The skin-name hides nothing and binds nothing.
  5. What it does. It addresses the person beneath every title, baptism and mask. This is why baptism cannot overwrite it. Vytautas is Wigand and then Aleksandras to the Church, but his true name is unchanged, and this is why the amber answers to his truth, not his creed (magic_rules §5.4). Under magic_rules §10, someone who knows your true name better than you do is beyond your workings.
  6. Hooks. The Shadow-Seller collects true names. A mother who dies in childbirth takes the name with her, and that child is unbindable, which is a blessing and a curse. When the Root-Mother takes Eglė, she names her anew (Pelkių Eglė), which is what “taken” means (D5).

  • Real officers of the period (from houses_and_lineages.md): Kuno von Hattenstein, Konrad von Wallenrode, Friedrich von Wallenrode, Marquard von Salzbach, Kuno von Liebenstein (the 1381 informant; Günther von Hohenstein is the saga’s invented young brother, sharing a name with an older real komtur), Thomas von Merheim, Werner von Tettingen, Wilhelm von Friemersheim.
  • Inventing a brother. Use Bruder + given name + von + a place in Franconia, Swabia, Thuringia, Hesse or the Rhineland (the Order’s recruiting grounds). Brethren were German-born; natives of Prussia were not admitted.
  • Offices (German, then Lithuanian): Hochmeister (didysis magistras), Großkomtur (didysis komtūras), Marschall (maršalas), Komtur (komtūras), Vogt (vogtas), Tressler (iždininkas).

6.5 Polish names [Record]

  • Men: Jan, Mikołaj, Piotr, Zbigniew, Spytek, Dobiesław, Krystyn, Sędziwój, Jaśko, Zawisza, Wojciech.
  • Women: Jadwiga, Elżbieta, Anna, Zofia, Katarzyna, Małgorzata.
  • Form: Jan z Tarnowa (“of Tarnów”); Lithuanian Jonas iš Tarnuvo.

6.6 Tatar names [Record]

Tokhtamysh, Edigu, Temur Qutlugh, Jalal ad-Din, Kadir Berdi, Hacı Giray, Mamai. Titles: khan, emir, beg, mirza, beglerbeg.
