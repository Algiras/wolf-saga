— Go into the forest, — he told them through his teeth. — Do not look back.

He went to the long storehouse. Under a single roof lay the beams of the siege towers, bombards, powder barrels and burning oil. In the daylight he had noticed the Germans' one mistake: the engineers, who had counted everything, had not counted that fire does not choose its master.

— Who's skulking there? — came a young, indifferent voice right by the door.

From behind the corner stepped a sentry, barely more than a boy, whose armour gleamed with newness even in the dark. He recognised the prince, and relief lit his face, not fear: he had been left on guard at the most important storehouse and believed it to be an honour.

— Prince, — he began.

Vytautas struck quickly and silently. It was not an honourable knightly duel of the kind the chronicles would one day describe, but simply murder in a dark courtyard, hasty and ugly. Warm blood spilled over his hands, sticky and just like that which had been on them in the fields of Trakai.

— Forgive me, brother, but your god is not here tonight, — he whispered, but the words drowned in the sounds of the night.

He entered the storehouse with a hidden torch. The powder barrels and little kegs of burning oil stood there. He closed his eyes for a moment and threw the fire.
