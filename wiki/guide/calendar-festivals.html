Calendar & festivals¶

How the year was counted in the 1380s: the old month names, the festival wheel, the saga’s magical zeniths and the Teutonic Order’s Christian calendar.

Labels. [Historical record] is attested in the sources or standard scholarship; [The saga’s invention] was made for this series.

A caution about “pagan” customs. Almost everything we know about Lithuanian festival customs was written down centuries after 1387: by 16th–17th-century churchmen, and above all by 19th–20th-century folklorists recording peasants who were Catholic. What those sources preserve is double faith, old practice under Christian names. How any of it looked in 1382 is unrecorded. The saga is entitled to its pre-Christian versions, but it should know that it is reconstructing them.

1. Counting time in the 1380s [Historical record]¶

1.1 The Julian calendar and the eight-day gap¶

Everyone who wrote dates in 1382 used the Julian calendar. In the 14th century it ran 8 days behind the sun as the modern (Gregorian) calendar measures it (for dates between 1 Mar 1300 and 28 Feb 1500, Julian + 8 = proleptic Gregorian).

Consequence for the saga. Christmas Eve (Kūčios, 24 Dec) fell about 11 days after the winter solstice, and St John’s Eve (Joninės, 23 June) about 10 days after the summer solstice. The folk equation “Kūčios = the solstice, Rasos = midsummer” is right in spirit, because the Church set its feasts on the old solstice festivals, but in the 1380s the days no longer matched.

Saga rule [The saga’s invention]: the Kalba zeniths are astronomical, because the elements watch the sky, not the Church calendar. In 1382 the pagan solstice night falls on about 13 December. Christianised households keep Kūčios on 24 December. After 1387 the feast drifts away from the zenith, and the night that families keep sacred is no longer the night the stars are strongest. It is a quiet image of the Unbinding, and Books II–V can use it.

1.2 How documents dated¶

  • Latin and German documents dated by feast days: in die Assumptionis beatae Mariae virginis (15 Aug), in vigilia Assumptionis (14 Aug), feria secunda post Michaelis (the Monday after Michaelmas). The year was Anno Domini. Chanceries in the Empire often began the year at Christmas; the Order’s practice should be checked.

  • Ruthenian documents used the Byzantine era from the Creation. AD 1382 is 6890. A Ruthenian chronicle entry for Kęstutis’s death reads “in the year 6890”.

  • The Krėva Act is dated 14 August 1385, the eve of the Assumption, and so the eve of the third anniversary of Kęstutis’s death in the same castle (master_chronology).

1.3 How Lithuanians counted¶

Pagan Lithuania wrote nothing down. It counted by moons (mėnuo means both “moon” and “month”), by seasons, by feasts and by work: rugiapjūtė (the rye harvest), linarūtė (flax-pulling), sėja (sowing). Whether a seven-day week was in general use among pagan Lithuanians is unknown. Where the week appears in older Lithuanian and in the dialects, its day names are Slavic loans [Historical record]: panedėlis (Monday), utarninkas (Tuesday), sereda (Wednesday), četvergas (Thursday), pėtnyčia (Friday), subata (Saturday), nedėlia (Sunday). The modern names (pirmadienis “first day” and so on) are 20th-century coinages. Kęstutis should not say penktadienis. If he names a day at all, it is a feast or a moon.

2. The months¶

The modern Lithuanian month names are old folk names, but they were standardised only around 1900. Before that, regions used different names, and some names sat a month earlier or later. None is attested from the 14th century. The saga may use them as the moon-names of the pagan year ([The saga’s invention convention]), knowing they are attested later.

3. Moon and sun¶

3.1 The moon [Historical record]¶

Lithuanian names the phases: jaunatis (new moon, “the young one”), priešpilnis (waxing, “before full”), pilnatis (full moon), delčia (waning). Folk practice recorded in the 19th–20th centuries sows in the waxing moon, fells timber and cuts hair in the waning moon, and does not start anything at the new moon. The Moon (Mėnuo) is a warrior and a faithless husband whom Perkūnas cut in half, which is why he wanes (Cosmology §3.1).

3.2 The saga’s reckoning [The saga’s invention]¶

  • The krivis of Šventaragis keeps the year. The year turns at the winter solstice, read from the sun’s rising point against the hills of the Neris valley. The year counts twelve moons from there.

  • The blind moon. Twelve lunar months fall about 11 days short of a solar year. When the twelfth moon dies too early, the krivis declares a thirteenth moon, aklasis mėnuo (“the blind moon”), in which no oath is sworn and no marriage made. (Name and rule are inventions. They give the saga a real-sounding lunisolar mechanism, since medieval Lithuania must have reconciled moons and seasons somehow, and nothing is recorded.)

  • After 1387 there is no krivis and no one to declare the blind moon. Peasants slowly adopt the Church calendar, and the old moon-year drifts. Another sign of the Unbinding.

  • The zeniths are astronomical (§1.1). The calendar of feasts is how people keep them, and the two need not coincide.

5. The Christian calendar overlay: the Order’s year [Historical record]¶

  • Two Reise seasons. The Order raided Lithuania mostly in winter (December–February, when swamps and rivers froze into roads) and in late summer (August–September, after the harvest). Western “guests” (French, English, Bohemian knights) came for them. By custom the Reisen were tied to the great Marian feasts: the Purification (2 Feb) for the winter season and the Assumption (15 Aug) for the summer. A banquet of honour, the Ehrentisch (Table of Honour), was sometimes held before a Reise.

  • Mud seasons. Spring thaw (March–April) and autumn rains (October–November) stopped armies. The “two muddy seasons” are why Book I’s set pieces cluster in high summer and deep winter.

  • Marian feasts (the Order’s patroness is Mary): Purification 2 Feb, Annunciation 25 Mar, Assumption 15 Aug, Nativity of Mary 8 Sept. (The Visitation, 2 July, was only instituted in 1389; no Order knight keeps it in Book I.)

  • Dates that fall on feasts:

    • 15 Aug 1382: Kęstutis found dead at Krėva on the Assumption, the feast of the Order’s own patroness. The Lithuanian name Žolinė belongs to the Christian centuries; in the saga, the peasants who later keep Žolinė half-remember it as “the day the old duke died”.

    • 21 Oct 1383: Vytautas baptised Wigand at Tapiau on St Ursula’s Day.

    • 14 Aug 1385: the Krėva Act, on the eve of the Assumption.

    • 18 Feb and 4 Mar 1386: Jogaila’s wedding and coronation both fall in Shrovetide, the last days before Lent (Ash Wednesday was 7 Mar 1386), when weddings were still allowed. The haste of the Kraków calendar is visible in the dates.

    • Feb–Mar 1387: the baptism of Lithuania falls in Lent (Ash Wednesday 20 Feb 1387; Easter 7 Apr). Jogaila’s privilege to the Catholic boyars is usually dated 20 Feb 1387, which would be Ash Wednesday itself.

    • 15 Jul 1410: Žalgiris, on the feast of the Dispersion of the Apostles (Długosz notes it).
