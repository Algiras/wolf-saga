The castle was already filled with strange guests as well: merchants from Ryga and Danzig, smelling of spices and wax, with eyes that counted everything. One of them told Jogaila that the Polish lords had just crowned a child, Jadvyga, as their king and were looking for a husband for her, and Jogaila's hands suddenly stopped trembling. Another held out a Ryga silver coin to Vytautas so that he might examine the minting. The coin was warm, as if just taken from another person's palm. Vytautas put it back. Somewhere behind him, quietly, not meddling in the talk, someone was counting. It was Aukso Rijūnas. The merchants measured everything not by honour or by family tree but by the weight of the mark. The old order, in which a given word was sacred and the sword was the sign of truth, was withering under the weight of ledgers. Everything became a commodity, even God, whom Jogaila would soon be preparing to accept.

Jogaila drew a deep breath, feeling his lungs fill with the scent of exotic incense and the costliest Western waxes. It was a luxury that in Vilnius had become his everyday life, but which in Trakai, in the eyes of his parents, would have been counted the greatest sin. Silver was hidden in his pouches — the promises of Hanulas, the Ryga merchant, smelling of Western power and the new gods. That silver was heavy; it dragged him down, smothering the last remnants of respect for his own blood and for the traditions of his uncle Kęstutis. Jogaila never wondered why he had never once seen the Ryga merchant Hanulas and the jester Henė in the same room.
