The Ancient Speech

Lore of the Craft: how a working is asked

lore

“Do not command the well, child. Speak to it as you would to your grandmother’s ghost: by name, with your hands open, and say how you will repay it.”

[The saga’s invention] A Palanga priestess to a girl at the shrine, as remembered in the amber-keepers’ oral lessons.

An asking, not a command

The Senovės Kalba is not a spell-book language. In the saga it is Lithuanian in its oldest register, the words the elements were first addressed in. It was never written, only handed down from krivis to žynys and from mother to daughter. Mortals ask in it, and the elements answer only to it (Languages & naming, §4; Magic rules, §3).

Everything on this page is the saga’s invention. The historical record shows real Lithuanian folk charms (užkalbėjimai) and real prayers to Perkūnas, Žemyna and Gabija, but no five-element speech. The five lines are plain modern Lithuanian, as the language notes admit. See also Senovės Kalba.

The formula

Every working has three words in a fixed order: element, recipient, verb.

Element Line Plain sense
Stars (Praurimė) Žvaigždės tau švytės The stars will shine for you
Fire (Gabija) Ugnis tau degs Fire will burn for you
Water (Bangpūtys) Vanduo tau teks Water shall be your portion
Earth (Žemyna) Žemė tau laikys Earth will hold for you
Wind (Vėjopatis) Vėjas tau neš Wind will carry for you

The verb is last, in the third-person future. Grammatically a working is not a wish but a prophecy the caster asks the element to make true. Under the Law of Truth a false prophecy fails, which is why a caster cannot ask for what they do not truly intend. The water line carries a double sense: teks is “falls to one’s lot”, the fitting verb for the element that heals by taking the wound onto the healer.

Rules that behave like grammar

Rule What it means Seen in
Element first, in the nominative Naming the power is the address. Never reorder. Every working
tau (for you) or man (for me) Tau is cast on another; man on oneself, at lighter cost. Birutė, Book I ch. 3; Vytautas, ch. 15
Verb last, third person future The element’s own natural act: shines, burns, holds, carries. All five lines
Every line closes on a stressed syllable A hard, falling-shut cadence. švy-TĖS, DEGS, TEKS, lai-KYS, NEŠ
Spoken aloud, a whisper suffices The Order gags captured žyniai. Magic rules, §3.2
No Christian or Slavic loanwords The elements do not know them. Convention of the saga
Sealed with a cost clause The caster states, or at least accepts, the bill. Jo skola — mano skola

Not specified: a possible jam/jai (“for him/her”) and mums (“for us”) are proposed as extensions only; this page does not treat them as part of the speech.

The five voices

Spoken together, in the order Stars, Fire, Water, Earth, Wind, the five lines are the Five-Voice Invocation (Penkių Balsų Šauksmas). It calls a little of every boon and costs a little of every price, permanently. Birutė gives it to Vytautas in Book I ch. 3 with tau; he speaks it himself with man in ch. 15 and again in Book II ch. 27.

Names and the cost clause

Two further ingredients are not in the five lines. The first is the true name (slaptas vardas), the single old word a mother whispers at birth. A binder speaks it in a Sutartis, and it is why baptism cannot overwrite a man (Book I ch. 3). The second is the cost clause: Jo skola — mano skola (“his debt is my debt”), spoken in Book I ch. 3 not to the bound man but to “the room, the walls, the lake, the fire in the candle”, which is how one speaks to witnesses.

Why asking, not commanding

A command assumes the element owes obedience. The saga’s gods are facts of nature, not servants: Perkūnas cannot be asked at all, and a Force must be bargained with. The Kalba works because the mortal petitions politely and pays. This is the Law of Balance: Kiek paimi, tiek atiduok, “as much as you take, give back”. The cost is part of the grammar, not a penalty added afterwards. See The Price.
