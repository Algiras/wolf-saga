# How to Say It: A Pronunciation Guide

*Canonical reference for how every name and term in **Geležinio Vilko Saga** is spoken, in Lithuanian and in English narration. It sits below `canon_decisions.md` in precedence: if D4 changes a spelling, this file follows. Its machine-readable twin is `pronunciation_lexicon.json` (same rows, same stress), which the audiobook pipeline in `audiobook/` should use. The languages, name structures and the saga's "true names" are explained in `languages_and_naming.md`. Festival names are explained in `calendar_and_festivals.md`.*

> A name said wrongly is a small betrayal of the person who bore it. Vytautas was *VEE-tow-tahs* to everyone who ever spoke to him, and an audiobook that says *vy-TOW-tus* puts a stranger on his horse. The aim here is simple. A Lithuanian listener should hear the English narration and not wince.

---

## 1. How to use this guide

Each table row gives five things:

1. **Lithuanian spelling**, with full diacritics. Use this spelling in prose and docs; never strip the marks.
2. **Stress**, with the stressed syllable in CAPS (VY-tau-tas). This column is the one that matters most.
3. **IPA**, in broad transcription (conventions in §2).
4. **Sounds like**, an English sound-alike respelling (key in §4) for narrators who do not read IPA.
5. **Notes**: who or what it is, meaning or etymology where scholarship supports one, and the usual English mistake to avoid.

**Stress confidence.** Stress follows standard Lithuanian accentuation, the accent paradigms of the *Dabartinės lietuvių kalbos žodynas*, the VLKK names database and the *Lietuvių kalbos žodynas*. Where I am not confident of a word's stress, the row says **(verify stress)**. Do not record those rows until a Lithuanian speaker or one of those dictionaries has confirmed them. They are collected in the "Rows to verify" section near the end. The anchors supplied by the author (Výtautas, Jogáila, Kęstùtis, Birùtė, Gedìminas, Algìrdas, Vìlnius, Trakaĩ, Palangà, Perkū́nas, Žemýna, Eglė̃, Jū́ratė, Kastýtis) are used as given.

**Labels.** **[Record]** is attested in historical, linguistic or folklore sources (the date of the source is given where it matters). **[Saga invention]** was made for this series. **(verify)** is a fact to check before it goes into chapter text.

## 2. A Lithuanian sound primer for English speakers

Lithuanian is spelled almost exactly as it sounds. Once you know the letters, you can read any name aloud correctly except for one thing, the **stress**, which spelling does not show (§2.5).

### 2.1 The alphabet

Lithuanian has 32 letters. These are the ones that differ from English:

| Letter | IPA | Sounds like | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **a** | [ɐ] short, [aː] long | short: *u* in *cut*; long: *a* in *father* | *Trakai* (trah-KYE) |
| **ą** | [aː] | *a* in *father* (always long) | *Vytautą* (acc.) |
| **c** | [ts] | *ts* in *cats* | *Lucko* |
| **č** | [tʃ] | *ch* in *church* | *Kūčios* |
| **e** | [ɛ] short, [æː] long | short: *e* in *bet*; long: *a* in *bad*, drawn out | *Nemunas* (NEH-moo-nahs) |
| **ę** | [æː] | *a* in *bad*, drawn out (always long) | *Kęstutis* |
| **ė** | [eː] | *ay* in *day* with no glide, like French *é* held | *Birutė* |
| **g** | [ɡ] | always hard, as in *go*, never as in *gem* | *Gediminas* |
| **h** | [ɣ] | a voiced, soft *h*; an English *h* is acceptable (loanwords only) | *Hanulas* |
| **i** | [ɪ] | *i* in *bit*; between a consonant and a back vowel it is silent and only softens the consonant (§2.4) | *Vilnius* (VIL-nyoos) |
| **į**, **y** | [iː] | *ee* in *see* (always long). **Never "eye".** | *Vytautas*, *Įsrūtis* |
| **j** | [j] | *y* in *yes* | *Jogaila* (yo-GUY-lah) |
| **o** | [oː] | *o* in *more*, pure, no glide; short [ɔ] only in loanwords | *Jonas*, *Sofija* |
| **š** | [ʃ] | *sh* in *ship* | *Švitrigaila* |
| **u** | [ʊ] | *u* in *put* | *Kęstutis* |
| **ų**, **ū** | [uː] | *oo* in *moon* (always long) | *Trakų*, *Perkūnas* |
| **v** | [ʋ] | between *v* and *w*; an English *v* is fine | *Vilnius* |
| **ž** | [ʒ] | *s* in *measure* | *Žemyna* |
| **ch** | [x] | *ch* in Scottish *loch* (loanwords only) | — |
| **dž** | [dʒ] | *j* in *judge* | *Pradžia* |

The four "tailed" vowels **ą ę į ų** were nasal vowels centuries ago. Today they are simply **long**, and they mostly mark grammatical endings (*Vytautą* is the accusative). Never pronounce them nasal or silent.

### 2.2 Long and short vowels

- **Always short:** *a, e, i, u* (but see the next point).
- **Always long:** *ą, ę, ė, į, y, ų, ū*, and *o* in native words.
- **Stressed *a* and *e* in an open, non-final syllable become long:** *Nemunas* is [ˈnʲæːmʊnɐs], *ragana* is [ˈraːɡɐnɐ].
- **Unstressed vowels keep their quality.** Lithuanian has no "uh" (schwa). English speakers reduce every unstressed vowel to "uh"; do not. *Vytautas* ends in a clear *-tahs*, not *-tus*. *Žemyna* ends in *-nah*, not *-nuh*.

### 2.3 Diphthongs

| Spelling | IPA | Sounds like | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ai** | [ɐɪ] | *eye* | *Jogaila* (yo-GUY-lah) |
| **au** | [ɐʊ] | *ow* in *cow* | *Vytautas* (VEE-tow-tahs) |
| **ei** | [ɛɪ] | *ay* in *day* | *Medeina* (meh-DAY-nah) |
| **ie** | [iɛ] | *ee-eh* glided into **one** syllable | *Dievas* (DYEH-vahs) |
| **uo** | [uɔ] | *oo-oh* glided into **one** syllable | *Vanduo* (vahn-DWOH) |
| **ui** | [ʊɪ] | *ooy* as in *phooey* | (no saga name; common in *muilas* "soap") |

A vowel followed by *l, m, n, r* (*al, an, ar, il, ir, el, ul, ur…*) forms a **mixed diphthong**, a single long syllable that can carry the stress: *Al-GIR-das*, *KAL-nas*.

**Spellings with *i* before a vowel** (*ia, io, iu, ią, iū, iau*) are **not** two syllables. The *i* only softens the consonant before it: *Velnias* is VEL-nyahs (two syllables), *Vilnius* is VIL-nyoos (two), *Karaliaučius* is kah-rah-LYOW-chyoos (four).

### 2.4 Consonants and palatalisation

- **Hard and soft.** Almost every consonant comes in a hard and a soft (palatalised) version. A consonant is soft before *e, ę, ė, i, į, y*, before *ie* and *ei*, and before a silent *i* (*ia, iu, io*). To an English ear a soft consonant carries a faint *y*: *Kęstutis* [kʲæːs-], *Gediminas* [ɡʲɛ-]. IPA marks this with [ʲ].
- **Softness spreads backwards** through a cluster: *Vilnius* [ˈvʲɪlʲnʲʊs], *Algirdas* [ɐlʲˈɡʲɪrdɐs].
- **Voicing assimilates.** A voiced consonant before a voiceless one becomes voiceless, and the reverse: *Bangpūtys* [bɐŋkˈpuːtʲiːs], *degs* [dʲɛks], *išdavikas* [ɪʒdɐˈvʲɪkɐs]. A voiced consonant at the very end of a word is devoiced.
- **No puff of air** after *p, t, k* (as in French or Spanish).
- ***l*** is clear before front vowels and dark [ɫ] before back vowels and consonants (*Laima* [ˈɫɐɪmɐ], but *Velnias* [ˈvʲælʲnʲɐs]).
- ***r*** is tapped or trilled, and **always** sounded, including before consonants (*Algirdas*, *Jurbarkas*). Do not drop it the English way.
- ***n*** before *g* or *k* is [ŋ], as in *finger*: *Palanga* [pɐɫɐŋˈɡɐ], *Lengvenis*.

### 2.5 Stress: free and mobile

This is where English narration goes wrong, and it is the main reason this guide exists.

- **Free.** Lithuanian stress can fall on any syllable: **VY**-tau-tas, jo-**GAI**-la, tra-**KAI**, pa-lan-**GA**. Spelling never shows it. It must be learned **word by word**, exactly like the stress of English *photograph / photography*.
- **Mobile.** In many nouns the stress **moves** between case forms. *Eglė̃* is stressed on the ending, but the accusative *Ẽglę* is stressed on the stem. Lithuanian grammar sorts nouns into four **accent paradigms**. Paradigm 1 keeps the stress on the stem; paradigms 2–4 move it to the ending in some cases. §3 lists the saga names that move.
- **English instincts fail both ways.** English speakers stress the first syllable (wrong for *Palangà*, *Trakaĩ*, *Eglė̃*, *žaltỹs*, *Dubysà*) or the Latin penult (wrong for *Výtautas*, *Nẽmunas*, *Jū́ratė*). Check every name.
- **In phrases**, each noun and verb keeps its own stress. Short words (*tau, man, ir, į, už, kol*) are unstressed clitics.

### 2.6 Pitch accents (optional for English speakers)

On a stressed **long** syllable Lithuanian distinguishes two tones: **falling** (the acute, written ´: *Výtautas*) and **rising** (the circumflex, written ˜: *Trakaĩ*). A stressed **short** syllable takes the grave (`: *Gedìminas*). In mixed diphthongs beginning with *i* or *u*, dictionaries write the falling tone as a grave (*Algìrdas*). Dictionaries print these marks; ordinary text never does.

For English narration you may **ignore the tones**. Put the stress on the right syllable, keep the long vowels long and the soft consonants soft, and a Lithuanian listener will hear the name as correct. A native narrator will supply the tones without thinking. The tone marks appear in §3 because they are the standard way to show stress in Lithuanian.

### 2.7 Seven rules that fix most mistakes

1. **Stress the CAPS syllable, every time**, and do not shrink the other vowels to "uh".
2. **y and į = "ee"**, never "eye": VEE-tow-tahs, zheh-MEE-nah.
3. **j = "y"**: yo-GUY-lah, YOO-rah-tay.
4. **ė = "ay"**, and a final *-ė* is a full syllable: *Birutė* has three syllables (bih-ROO-tay), *Eglė* two (eg-LAY).
5. **i before a vowel is not a syllable**: VIL-nyoos, VEL-nyahs.
6. **š = sh, ž = zh, č = ch, c = ts, g is always hard.**
7. **ą ę į ų are long vowels**, not nasal and not silent: *Kęstutis* = kess-TOO-tis.

## 5. Method and sources

- **Stress:** standard Lithuanian accentuation. Check flagged rows against the *Dabartinės lietuvių kalbos žodynas*, the VLKK names database (*Vardynas*), the *Lietuvių kalbos žodynas*, or an automatic accentuator (e.g. VDU's *Kirčiuoklis*), and then confirm by ear with a native speaker.
- **IPA:** broad. [ɐ] is short *a*, [aː] long *a*, [ɛ] short *e*, [æː] long *e/ę*, [eː] *ė*, [ɪ] short *i*, [iː] *y/į*, [oː] *o*, [ʊ] short *u*, [uː] *ū/ų*. [ʲ] marks palatalisation and [ɫ] dark *l*. [v] stands for the Lithuanian labiodental approximant [ʋ], and [ɣ] for *h*. Unstressed native *o* is written [o].
- **Foreign names** are given in their native pronunciation, with the Lithuanian form in the notes. Lithuanian forms of foreign names that I could not confirm are marked (verify).
- **Etymologies** are given only where they are widely accepted. Uncertain ones are called uncertain, and none is invented.

## 19. Notes for the audiobook pipeline

- `pronunciation_lexicon.json` holds **351 entries** with these fields:
  - `text`: canonical spelling.
  - `variants`: inflected Lithuanian forms that occur in prose.
  - `ipa`, `respelling_en` and `stress`: as in this guide.
  - `category`: `character`, `foreign`, `deity`, `spirit`, `place`, `term`, `incantation`, `saying` or `title`.
  - `verify`: `true` means do not trust the stress yet.
  - `ascii` (where it differs): the diacritic-stripped forms the current pipeline produces (*Kestutis*, *Shvitrigaila*, *Zhemyna*), so that they can still be matched.
  - `stress_notes` (on names whose stress moves): which case forms shift.
- **The English pipeline currently destroys the information it needs.** `audiobook/1_preprocess_with_ollama.py` (lines 200–205) tells the model to strip *ą ę į ų ū ė* and turn *š č ž* into *sh ch zh*. After that, *ę* and *ė* (different vowels) both become *e*, and nothing marks stress. The prompt also contradicts itself: line 204 says "zh for ž", but line 205's example gives *Žemyna → Zemyna*. **Better:** keep the Lithuanian spelling through translation, then substitute from the lexicon. Use SSML `<phoneme alphabet="ipa" ph="…">` where the TTS engine supports it, or the `respelling_en` text where it does not. Match longest strings first, so that *Šešėlių Pardavėjas* wins over *Pardavėjas*.
- **Variants matter mostly for a Lithuanian narration.** The English translation will mostly contain nominatives (and English possessives, *Vytautas's*). For a Lithuanian voice, every form in `variants` keeps the nominative's stressed syllable, except where `stress_notes` says otherwise.
- **Respellings are for English voices.** For a Lithuanian voice, feed the IPA (or the dictionary accent marks from §3), not the respelling.
