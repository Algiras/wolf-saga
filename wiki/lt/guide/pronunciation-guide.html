Pronunciation guide¶

How every name and term in the saga is spoken, in Lithuanian and in English narration.

Labels. [Historical record] is attested in the sources or standard scholarship; [The saga’s invention] was made for this series.

A name said wrongly is a small betrayal of the person who bore it. Vytautas was VEE-tow-tahs to everyone who ever spoke to him, and an audiobook that says vy-TOW-tus puts a stranger on his horse. The aim here is simple. A Lithuanian listener should hear the English narration and not wince.

1. How to use this guide¶

Each table row gives five things:

  1. Lithuanian spelling, with full diacritics. Use this spelling in prose and docs; never strip the marks.

  2. Stress, with the stressed syllable in CAPS (VY-tau-tas). This column is the one that matters most.

  3. IPA, in broad transcription (conventions in §2).

  4. Sounds like, an English sound-alike respelling (key in §4) for narrators who do not read IPA.

  5. Notes: who or what it is, meaning or etymology where scholarship supports one, and the usual English mistake to avoid.

Stress confidence. Stress follows standard Lithuanian accentuation, the accent paradigms of the Dabartinės lietuvių kalbos žodynas, the VLKK names database and the Lietuvių kalbos žodynas. Where I am not confident of a word’s stress, the row says. Do not record those rows until a Lithuanian speaker or one of those dictionaries has confirmed them. The anchors supplied by the author (Výtautas, Jogáila, Kęstùtis, Birùtė, Gedìminas, Algìrdas, Vìlnius, Trakaĩ, Palangà, Perkū́nas, Žemýna, Eglė̃, Jū́ratė, Kastýtis) are used as given.

The four „tailed“ vowels ą ę į ų were nasal vowels centuries ago. Today they are simply long, and they mostly mark grammatical endings (Vytautą is the accusative). Never pronounce them nasal or silent.

2.2 Long and short vowels¶

  • Always short: a, e, i, u (but see the next point).

  • Always long: ą, ę, ė, į, y, ų, ū, and o in native words.

  • Stressed a and e in an open, non-final syllable become long: Nemunas is [ˈnʲæːmʊnɐs], ragana is [ˈraːɡɐnɐ].

  • Unstressed vowels keep their quality. Lithuanian has no „uh“ (schwa). English speakers reduce every unstressed vowel to „uh“; do not. Vytautas ends in a clear -tahs, not -tus. Žemyna ends in -nah, not -nuh.

A vowel followed by l, m, n, r (al, an, ar, il, ir, el, ul, ur…) forms a mixed diphthong, a single long syllable that can carry the stress: Al-GIR-das, KAL-nas.

Spellings with i before a vowel (ia, io, iu, ią, iū, iau) are not two syllables. The i only softens the consonant before it: Velnias is VEL-nyahs (two syllables), Vilnius is VIL-nyoos (two), Karaliaučius is kah-rah-LYOW-chyoos (four).

2.4 Consonants and palatalisation¶

  • Hard and soft. Almost every consonant comes in a hard and a soft (palatalised) version. A consonant is soft before e, ę, ė, i, į, y, before ie and ei, and before a silent i (ia, iu, io). To an English ear a soft consonant carries a faint y: Kęstutis [kʲæːs-], Gediminas [ɡʲɛ-]. IPA marks this with [ʲ].

  • Softness spreads backwards through a cluster: Vilnius [ˈvʲɪlʲnʲʊs], Algirdas [ɐlʲˈɡʲɪrdɐs].

  • Voicing assimilates. A voiced consonant before a voiceless one becomes voiceless, and the reverse: Bangpūtys [bɐŋkˈpuːtʲiːs], degs [dʲɛks], išdavikas [ɪʒdɐˈvʲɪkɐs]. A voiced consonant at the very end of a word is devoiced.

  • No puff of air after p, t, k (as in French or Spanish).

  • l is clear before front vowels and dark [ɫ] before back vowels and consonants (Laima [ˈɫɐɪmɐ], but Velnias [ˈvʲælʲnʲɐs]).

  • r is tapped or trilled, and always sounded, including before consonants (Algirdas, Jurbarkas). Do not drop it the English way.

  • n before g or k is [ŋ], as in finger: Palanga [pɐɫɐŋˈɡɐ], Lengvenis.

2.5 Stress: free and mobile¶

This is where English narration goes wrong, and it is the main reason this guide exists.

  • Free. Lithuanian stress can fall on any syllable: VY-tau-tas, jo-GAI-la, tra-KAI, pa-lan-GA. Spelling never shows it. It must be learned word by word, exactly like the stress of English photograph / photography.

  • Mobile. In many nouns the stress moves between case forms. Eglė̃ is stressed on the ending, but the accusative Ẽglę is stressed on the stem. Lithuanian grammar sorts nouns into four accent paradigms. Paradigm 1 keeps the stress on the stem; paradigms 2–4 move it to the ending in some cases. §3 lists the saga names that move.

  • English instincts fail both ways. English speakers stress the first syllable (wrong for Palangà, Trakaĩ, Eglė̃, žaltỹs, Dubysà) or the Latin penult (wrong for Výtautas, Nẽmunas, Jū́ratė). Check every name.

  • In phrases, each noun and verb keeps its own stress. Short words (tau, man, ir, į, už, kol) are unstressed clitics.

2.6 Pitch accents (optional for English speakers)¶

On a stressed long syllable Lithuanian distinguishes two tones: falling (the acute, written ´: Výtautas) and rising (the circumflex, written ˜: Trakaĩ). A stressed short syllable takes the grave (`: Gedìminas). In mixed diphthongs beginning with i or u, dictionaries write the falling tone as a grave (Algìrdas). Dictionaries print these marks; ordinary text never does.

For English narration you may ignore the tones. Put the stress on the right syllable, keep the long vowels long and the soft consonants soft, and a Lithuanian listener will hear the name as correct. A native narrator will supply the tones without thinking. The tone marks appear in §3 because they are the standard way to show stress in Lithuanian.

2.7 Seven rules that fix most mistakes¶

  1. Stress the CAPS syllable, every time, and do not shrink the other vowels to „uh“.

  2. y and į = „ee“, never „eye“: VEE-tow-tahs, zheh-MEE-nah.

  3. j = „y“: yo-GUY-lah, YOO-rah-tay.

  4. ė = „ay“, and a final -ė is a full syllable: Birutė has three syllables (bih-ROO-tay), Eglė two (eg-LAY).

  5. i before a vowel is not a syllable: VIL-nyoos, VEL-nyahs.

  6. š = sh, ž = zh, č = ch, c = ts, g is always hard.

  7. ą ę į ų are long vowels, not nasal and not silent: Kęstutis = kess-TOO-tis.
