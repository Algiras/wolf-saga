The Iron Wolves

Source: world_rules/iron_wolves.md

Canonical for the sworn wolf-brotherhood. Subordinate to canon_decisions.md, cosmology.md, magic_rules.md and master_chronology.md. It is consistent with power_systems.md (§3, the Wolf-Change; §5, Kęstutis’s silver wolf and the Iron Wolf’s skull) and bestiary.md (§12 werewolves; §23 the Iron Wolf omen). Labels: [Record] means historical or folkloric fact; [Saga invention] means ours.

In one line. The vilkatai are men (and, late, a few women) who give their names to the sacred fire and their bodies to the bog, and in return can see what priests cannot and do what villages cannot. Before 1387 they are the Pact’s teeth. After 1387 they are the only thing standing between the peasantry and its hungry dead, and everyone who needs them also hates them.

1. At a glance

What peasants call them vilkatai (werewolves), or “the grey ones”, never to their faces
What they call themselves Dievo šunys, “God’s dogs”: the old folk name for wolves [Record], worn as a joke and a creed
What the Order calls them Wolfsbrüder, wolf-brothers [Saga invention]; heathen werewolves, with a bounty on their hides
The brotherhood Brolija; each band is a gauja (pack) of five to nine
Sworn to The Root-Mother (body) and the Gediminid flame (name). See §3
Numbers About 100 under Gediminas; about 120 in 1382; about 40 after the 1382 schism; a dozen by 1435; one (the Flame-Keeper) by the late 1440s [Saga invention]
Country The Dykra (geography_gazetteer.md B.2): bog-island lairs (guoliai) reached by sunken paths
Power family Granted power, not asked (power_systems.md §1). They are not casters and sit outside the practitioner tiers

2. What the record gives us [Record]

The brotherhood is invented, but every piece of it stands on something real.

  • The Indo-European wolf-warrior band. Scholars of early Indo-European society describe bands of young unmarried men who lived outside the settlement for a period, raided, and were ritually identified with wolves or dogs. This is the Männerbund (see Kris Kershaw, The One-Eyed God: Odin and the (Indo-)Germanic Männerbünde, 2000). Parallels often cited include the Old Norse úlfheðnar (“wolf-skins”, named beside the berserkers in skaldic verse) and the Irish fían war-bands. David Anthony and Dorcas Brown have interpreted midwinter dog and wolf sacrifices at the Bronze Age site of Krasnosamarskoe (Samara valley) as traces of such an initiation. The Proto-Indo-European name often reconstructed for the band is kóryos.
  • Baltic werewolves. Lithuanian folklore knows the vilkatas and vilkolakis (bestiary.md §12). Herodotus (Histories 4.105) says the Neuri, usually placed in this region, became wolves for a few days each year. Olaus Magnus (1555) describes werewolves gathering on Christmas night in Prussia, Livonia and Lithuania, breaking into cellars and drinking the beer.
  • The “hounds of God”. In 1692 at Jürgensburg in Livonia, an old man named Thiess told his judges that werewolves were the hounds of God. In his account they went down to hell three times a year to fight the witches and bring back the stolen seed-grain, so that the harvest would be good. Carlo Ginzburg made the case famous. Lithuanian peasants likewise called wolves Dievo šunys, God’s dogs, and folk custom made St Jurgis (George) the shepherd of wolves, feeding them on his feast day.
  • The Iron Wolf. Gediminas’s dream, interpreted by Lizdeika, is a 16th-century chronicle legend (legends_and_songs.md §3a).
  • Kęstutis’s war. Kęstutis fought the Teutonic Order on the western border for some four decades. He was captured in 1361 and escaped from Marienburg (master_chronology.md).
  • The Wildnis. A deliberately depopulated border belt crossed by raiders and by Lithuanian and Prussian guides (Wegeleute) whom the Order paid to lead its columns (geography_gazetteer.md B.2). Sudovia (Yotvingia) was conquered and largely emptied by 1283.
  • Sunken paths. Baltic peoples built kūlgrindos: paved causeways laid just below the water of a bog, invisible to strangers and used for defence.

3. Origins [Saga invention]

3.1 Before Gediminas: the Sudovian wolves

When the Order emptied Sudovia (1277–83), the last Sudovian warriors went into the new wilderness instead of into baptism or exile. They lived as wolf-bands in the bogs, raiding both sides. The Root-Mother, newly formed out of Žemyna’s and Medeina’s grief (cosmology.md §3.1), took some of them: the first men to lie down in the Dykra mud and get up changed. They had no master and no name. To the Lithuanians who traded with them, they were simply vilkatai.

3.2 Lizdeika’s hundred

The night of Gediminas’s dream, Lizdeika understood something he never told the Grand Duke (legends_and_songs.md §3b). The Iron Wolf was collared because the house would devour itself, and a collar needs a hand on the leash. In the morning he climbed the hill and found a wolf’s skull lying where the dream-wolf had stood, grown hard and grey as iron (Geležinio Vilko kaukolė, power_systems.md §5). He carried it into the Dykra and made the wolf-bands an offer. They would be given a place in the land’s order, food, honour and a fire to give their names to. In return they would guard the Gediminid hearth and the Pact’s peace, and they would be the house’s leash. A hundred came. That is the “hundred voices” in the folk version, read by the brotherhood their own way.

From then on, the brotherhood belonged to two masters: the Root-Mother, who made their bodies, and the Gediminid flame at Šventaragis, which kept their names. That double loyalty is the source of every tragedy below.

3.3 Kęstutis’s wolves (1345–1382)

After the Silent Pact of 1345 (cosmology.md §4.1), the brotherhood became the Pact’s teeth. Kęstutis, holding the west from Trakai, used them as his border raiders in the Wildnis. They burned Order supply-dumps, drowned Reise columns in the bogs, and guided Lithuanian raids into Prussia along routes no Wegeleute knew. He also kept a hidden guard of nine at his side, never named in any chronicle. Algirdas in Vilnius kept the other half of the brotherhood near the Šventaragis flame. When Algirdas died in 1377 those Vilnius packs passed to Jogaila with the city. This split is the root of the 1382 schism (§10).

4. Vilkų krikštas: the Wolves’ Baptism [Saga invention]

The rite is called krikštas, “baptism”, in mockery: the brotherhood took the Christian word and hung it on the oldest thing in the land. It is held in the twelve nights after Kūčios (the winter solstice), the time when the boundary between the living and the dead is thinnest and wolves are said to gather (compare Olaus Magnus and Krasnosamarskoe, §2).

Who is chosen. Unmarried men, usually 16 to 25 (the one exception is the Flame-Keeper, sworn at fourteen, nearly fifteen, in 1405): younger sons, outlaws, the last of burned villages, the occasional boyar’s bastard. Each must be sponsored by a sworn wolf (his vilktėvis, “wolf-father”), who answers for him with his own life.

The rite, in nine steps.

  1. The funeral feast. The candidate’s family holds a šermenys (wake) for him as though he had died. He eats his last meal at his mother’s hearth. From then on he is dead to the household, and his kin may not speak his name.
  2. The name to the fire. At a sacred flame (Šventaragis before 1387; Vytautas’s secret flame after), the candidate whispers his true name (magic_rules.md §6) into the fire. The fire keeps it. His name will go up to Dausos when he dies, without him.
  3. The throat-mark. The sponsor draws a shallow cut across the candidate’s throat at the voice-box and rubs live ash from that same flame into it. It heals as a thin pale line, the gerklės žymė. It is the only mark the brotherhood bears.
  4. Nine nights without fire. The candidate is taken into the Dykra naked but for a belt and a knife of bog-iron. For nine nights he may not light or approach a fire. The packs hunt him. If they catch him, they beat him and let him go; if he is caught three times, he is sent home, nameless and disgraced. Some do not survive the cold.
  5. The drowning. On the ninth night he is led along a kūlgrinda to the oldest bog in the Dykra and pressed under the mud until he stops struggling. The Root-Mother decides. Most come up. Some do not, and they are left where they lie. What comes up has left part of its soul below (power_systems.md §3).
  6. The oath on the skull. Dripping and blue with cold, he swears the Pack’s Word (§6) with both hands on the Iron Wolf’s skull. A lie spoken over the skull is heard by every wolf in the Dykra (power_systems.md §5).
  7. First meat. He eats raw from the pack’s kill, in silence, beside the others.
  8. The skin-name. The Vilkų Tėvas gives him a kailio vardas (skin-name): a nickname, a dead enemy’s name, or an object (Rūdis, Kirmėlė, Jonas). He answers to nothing else for the rest of his life.
  9. The wolf-hide. He receives a wolf-hide from his sponsor, from a wolf the sponsor killed with his own hands. He will be laid in the bog in it.

Failure. About a third of candidates are sent home or die [Saga invention]. The families of those sent home have already held their funeral, so the failures live as ghosts in their own villages. Some become the hedge-folk who later help the brotherhood; some become its informers.

5. What it makes of a man

5.1 Gifts (the Wolf-Change, power_systems.md §3)

  • The sight of the dvasios. The most important gift and the reason for everything after 1387: a vilkatas sees and hears spirits: laumės at the river, a slogutis on a sleeper’s chest, the hunger in an unfed grave. Priests cannot see them (cosmology.md §4.3).
  • Night-sight and scent. He sees by starlight as a man sees at dusk, and he can track by smell across a bog a day old.
  • The Root-Mother’s healing. Wounds packed with bog-moss and mud close in days instead of weeks; their blood is dark, runs slow, and smells of peat. This healing does not work on wounds made by feast-day iron.
  • Cold and wet are nothing. He can sleep in snow and lie in bog-water for hours.
  • Pack-sense. Sworn wolves know when a brother dies, wherever they are: they hear one more voice in the Iron Wolf’s chorus.
  • The iron hour (geležinė valanda). A battle-frenzy like the berserkers’ or úlfheðnar’s. For about an hour the wolf feels no pain or fear, and fights at a ferocity that has given the folk their tales of men becoming wolves. They do not change shape (see §5.4).

5.2 Flaws

  • No hearth. A vilkatas cannot sleep under a roof with a lit hearth. The fire that holds his name rejects his body, and he wakes choking, with a slogutis’s weight on his chest. They sleep in barns, byres and under the sky.
  • Feast-day iron burns them like any spirit (bestiary.md preface). Smith’s iron stings; bog-iron does not.
  • Consecrated stone sickens them: dizziness, nausea, their gifts dimmed (compare magic_rules.md §9).
  • The hungry dead hunt them (power_systems.md §3). A vilkatas smells of the roots, where the dead are going, and unfed vėlės are drawn to him like moths to a lantern. After 1387 this is both their worst danger and their main tool (§8.3).
  • The iron hour costs memory. Each frenzy takes a human memory: a face, a song, the way home. Old wolves are people with holes in them.
  • They cannot weep.

5.3 Physical tells (for prose)

The thin pale throat-scar. Cold skin even in summer. Dogs bristle and whine around them, and a farm-dog will not stay in a room with one (ch. 15–16’s fleeing hounds). A faint smell of peat and wet fur. Old wolves’ eye-teeth lengthen and their body-hair coarsens. No wolf-eyes, no glowing: they look like hard, weathered, quiet men.

5.4 The folk are half right: do they change?

Peasants are sure the vilkatai become wolves. In the iron hour they do not. But the Seniai (§7), wolves who have lived past twenty years sworn, can put on the wolf at need: real flesh, granted by the Root-Mother. Each time they come back less. The last time, they do not come back at all, and they run into the Dykra on four feet. Galminas’s beast-rage (persona/Galminas/) is a different thing again: the Root-Mother’s direct possession of her host, not the brotherhood’s gift.

5.5 The price under the Law of Balance

The Wolf-Change is granted power, bound by the Root-Mother’s nature rather than by the Three Laws. Even so, “Kiek paimi, tiek atiduok” still holds, because the Root-Mother keeps strict accounts.

Leaving. A vilkatas can return to human life once, if someone who knew his true name speaks it to him by a living fire and offers him bread on a knife-point (the folk cure for werewolves, half right as always). He loses every gift at once, ages the years he has spent sworn in a single winter, and the brotherhood treats him as dead. It is rare, and it is almost always a mother.

6. Gaujos žodis: the Pack’s Word [Saga invention]

Nine rules, sworn on the skull.

  1. Duona už duoną (“Bread for bread”). Take nothing that was not given. Pay for every meal, with meat, labour or blood.
  2. No Gediminid blood. A sworn wolf may not shed the blood of Gediminas’s house. This is the Pact’s second clause in the brotherhood’s own mouth. Breaking it gives the wolf to the Root-Mother at once: he goes into the bog alive.
  3. No sleep under a lit roof.
  4. The name stays in the fire. Never seek your own true name; never speak another wolf’s.
  5. Žaltys, oak and flame are never harmed.
  6. The dead go to the bog. A wolf is never burned and never buried in a churchyard. His brothers carry him home to the Dykra, however far.
  7. Courtesy before iron. With spirits, ask first, bargain second, kill last.
  8. Hunt what custom cannot hold. When the village’s charms, milk and salt fail, the wolf comes.
  9. The pack answers the flame, not the man. The brotherhood serves whoever keeps the Gediminid flame, whatever they think of him.

Rule 9 and rule 2 together make the brotherhood’s tragedy. In 1382 the Šventaragis flame stands in Jogaila’s Vilnius, so by the letter the brotherhood owes Jogaila. And no wolf may lift a hand against Skirgaila, whatever he does.

8. Weapons and craft

8.1 Arms

Light gear for bog-country: no plate, little mail. Wolf-hide over quilted wool or leather. Their weapons are bog-iron spears, heavy fighting knives and axes, hunting bows, and crossbows taken from the Order. They fight from ambush, at night, in water and reeds, and they prefer drowning a knight in a bog to fighting him on dry ground.

8.2 Bog-craft

They know every kūlgrinda in the Dykra and have laid many themselves. Lairs (guoliai) are bog-islands reached only by these paths. Signals are howls (a whole vocabulary, which the Order’s guides learned to fear) and marks cut in birch-bark. They speak with Wegeleute on both sides, and some of them are Wegeleute (§13, Rūdis).

8.3 The spirit-hunter’s kit (after 1387)

Each pack carries a salt horn (bought dear from Hanseatic smugglers), linen thread spun by the wolves’ sisters, a poppy-seed pouch, juniper, a thunder-stone if they can find one, the ash-pot, flint and steel, and a pyre-axe.

Their method is grim and practical, and it turns their worst flaw into a tool.

  1. Find it. They read the signs (bestiary.md) and talk to the village, the ragana and the dead.
  2. Ask. Most spirits can be bargained with (rule 7). Most hunts end here, with a feast for a forgotten grandmother or a žaltys carried to the forest.
  3. Bait. If asking fails, a wolf offers himself: the hungry dead are drawn to him (§5.2). He lies down inside a ring of live ash beside a prepared pyre.
  4. Burn. When the thing comes, the pack drags the body it rose from onto the pyre. Burning is the only true cure for the unburned (bestiary.md §16, §24).
  5. Leave before dawn, because after 1387 a pyre with a corpse on it is heresy, and someone always sees the smoke.

9. How they are seen

  • Peasants. Fear and need in equal measure. Mothers threaten children with them (feeding the baubas, bestiary.md §10). Yet the same mothers leave bread on a stump at the forest edge for them, as for wolves at Jurginės. After 1387 a village that needs them hangs a wolf’s jaw on its gatepost, and pays in bread, a night in the barn and silence. Nobody thanks them, and nobody tells the priest.
  • Boyars. Useful dogs: raiders, scouts and assassins of last resort. They are an embarrassment once the boyar is baptised, but boyars hire them secretly when the family’s own dead come for them.
  • Pagan priests (žyniai, the Krivis). Distrust. The vilkatai belong to the roots, the priests to the sky and fire. A man who is never burned is, to a žynys, half impure: his name goes up and he goes down. The Krivis keeps their names in his flame and does not invite them to his table. After 1387 the surviving žyniai and the wolves need each other and still do not like each other.
  • Christian clergy. Werewolves and devil-worshippers. The Order pays a bounty per hide. Yet the clergy are blind to the unbound spirits, and from Book III some Franciscans and parish priests quietly send for the wolves when their parishioners start dying. A medieval pun called the Dominicans Domini canes, “the Lord’s dogs” [Record]. The saga should let a friar notice that the Lord now has two kinds of dog.
  • The Order. The one enemy that does not fear them, because the Iron Saint grants its knights fearlessness (magic_rules.md §9). Wolves who face knights die like anyone else.
  • Vytautas. See §11.

10. Before the Unbinding: border raiders and the hidden guard (to 1387)

Until 1382 the brotherhood does three jobs: border war in the Wildnis for Kęstutis; guard (Kęstutis’s hidden nine; the Vilnius packs at the Šventaragis flame); and custom-keeping among the peasantry. That last job is the one the saga’s grey tone needs. A man who killed a žaltys or felled a sacred oak could expect a visit at night. The wolves were the Pact’s police, and the villages feared them for it.

The 1382 schism (Skilimas). When Jogaila took Vilnius in 1382, the Vilnius packs under their leader Kalvis (§13) obeyed rule 9: the flame was in Jogaila’s city, so they owed Jogaila. They did not kill Kęstutis’s men. They stood aside while Skirgaila’s killers did their work at Jogaila’s camp near Trakai (ch. 7), and they told themselves that this kept rule 2. Kęstutis’s packs went with their prince; seven of the hidden nine died in the camp slaughter and were buried in the bog, while Jonas’s men stayed in Kęstutis’s own camp and were not in the tents. The brotherhood has been at war with itself ever since. Wolves have eaten wolves, as ch. 21 puts it (“Vilkas pradėjo ėsti savo paties gaują”).

11. After the Unbinding: the only ones who hunt (1387–1450)

In 1387 Jogaila puts out the Šventaragis flame (ch. 29). In one stroke:

  • The Vilnius packs’ master broke the Pact himself, and their loyalty to Jogaila is void.
  • The only Gediminid flame left is the one Vytautas lights in secret that night (magic_rules.md §6). By rule 9, the whole brotherhood now answers to Vytautas, including the men who held the doors at his father’s murder.
  • New initiations need a kept flame. Every future wolf must be named into Vytautas’s fire, so the brotherhood’s survival is tied to his. When his flame dies in 1430, so does the krikštas.
  • Live ash becomes priceless. It is the one ward that holds the unburned dead and calms the Quenched (bestiary.md §27). The ash-bearers make a dangerous journey to Trakai to refill their pots.

From then on the vilkatai are what cosmology.md §4.3 promises: the feared, needed hunters of unbound spirits. They are hated by the Church, used by boyars, paid by peasants in bread and silence, and dying out.

12. The wolves and Vytautas, Books I–V

12.1 Why he is never one of them

Vytautas is bound by his mother’s Sutartis and claimed by Perkūnas (magic_rules.md §2, §7). The Root-Mother cannot take a man already owned twice. Claims can be contested, and she would lose. So the wolves follow a man who is not a wolf. They call him Vilkų kunigaikštis, prince of the wolves, half in irony.

12.3 Books II–V

  • Book II (1387–99). The wolves guard the secret flame in the Trakai island vault. Vytautas must judge Kalvis (he spares him, and the old Kęstutis wolves never forgive either of them). 1392: Šešiapirštis goes into Prussia to save Vytautas’s hostage sons, finds them already poisoned (herb-lore, mundane: power_systems.md §2.4), and comes back to lay himself in the bog. 1399, Vorskla: the wolves will not follow Vytautas into the steppe, because the Root-Mother’s gifts fade far from forest. The few who go die. “No mother’s fog, only shame” (magic_rules.md §4.3), and no wolves either.
  • Book III (1399–1411). The Samogitian uprisings (1401, 1409) beside Galminas. On campaign an ash-bearer carries the secret flame in a lantern (books/series_arc.md, Book III). Grunwald, 1410: the wolves are Vytautas’s vanguard, and they run in the Lithuanian retreat, real or feigned (master_chronology.md), leading pursuing knights into the woods. The Riders of the Reise scream when the Iron Saint breaks (bestiary.md §25).
  • Book IV (1411–30). The Hidden Groves fall, and with Samogitia baptised the Church hunts the wolves in earnest. A second split: the krikštyti vilkai (baptised wolves) buy their lives by taking bishops’ coin to hunt the unbound in parishes, and by informing on the rest, while Pilkoji’s faction keeps the old way. Henė’s offer (the crown for the flame, magic_rules.md §6) threatens the brotherhood’s existence. If Vytautas lets the flame die, there will be no more wolves, and rule 2 forbids them to stop him by force. The key Book IV wolf scene: Pilkoji at the vault door, deciding whether to break rule 2. 1430: Vytautas dies and the flame goes out.
  • Book V (1430–50). See §14.

13. Notable members [Saga invention]

Skin-names are given; birth-names were pagan two-part names (in the style of Daugirdas, Rambaudas: houses_and_lineages.md) and are lost, by rule 4.

  • Šešiapirštis (Six-Fingers), Vilkų Tėvas, c. 1325–1392. Grandson of Sudovian wolves; sworn in the year of the Pact. Kęstutis’s oldest captain, who knew the man and not the prince. Dry, patient, merciless to his own. Book I: unseen in the Dykra (ch. 15–16); he meets Vytautas only after ch. 25, at the hunters’ hut. Dies in Book II.
  • Jonas, c. 1335–1384. Kęstutis’s armour-bearer and the vilkų vyresnysis (pack-elder) of the hidden nine, and so of the ch. 25 band (books/character_arcs.md §5). His skin-name is the baptismal name of an Order sergeant he killed in 1361 during Kęstutis’s escape from Marienburg: the brotherhood’s grim habit of wearing the enemy’s Christian name as a trophy. This explains why a pagan wolf has a saint’s name (ch. 25:7). He survives ch. 7, leaves the horse in ch. 15, and dies in ch. 25.
  • Kirmėlė (the Worm). One of the hidden nine. In 1382 he sold the hidden guard’s sleeping-place to Skirgaila through Pinčiukas (bestiary.md §21) to save his home village from burning; he was not in the tent that night. The village was burned anyway. He is exposed in Book II (ch. 11, the Trakai vault; Henė, Pušis), and Vytautas lets him live, because he needs every wolf. Grey to the bone.
  • Rūdis (Rust). A Sambian Prussian, an Order Wegeleute guide of long standing, and (in the canon, not yet on the page in Book I) a sworn wolf: the brotherhood’s eyes inside the Order. Book I ch. 17 shows only his recognition of the silver wolf (the “nineteen of twenty years” figure is unsupported by the text); a double agent through Books II–III; dies at Grunwald in Order colours, killed by a Lithuanian who never knew.
  • Kalvis (the Smith), leader of the Vilnius packs. He held the doors in 1382 by the letter of rule 9. He guards the lodge flame from 1387 (it is Vytautas, not Kalvis, who carries the ember out: D11) and asks Vytautas for judgement. Book II: spared, despised, loyal unto death.
  • Pilkoji (the Grey One), born c. 1365 (Book III ch. 7 has her 38 in 1404; Book V ch. 28 has her 84 in 1449; Q13 made her older than the Flame-Keeper). A Samogitian woman of thirty-eight whose village’s dead rose in 1404. She walked into the Dykra, and the Root-Mother took her into the krikštas against all custom: after the Unbinding, custom no longer binds even the Root-Mother. The first woman sworn. Books III–V; the only Vilkų Motina.
  • The Flame-Keeper (skin-name Žiežirba, D11), born c. 1390–91 (5 in winter 1395–96, 14 and nearly 15 at Kūčios 1405, 48 in 1438; Books II–V). Book V’s protagonist (books/character_arcs.md §4): a boy rescued during the first Unbinding hunts of Book II (winter 1395–96), raised in the Trakai vault as the secret flame’s guard (a boy tending the flame from 1399), and sworn at Kūčios 1405 at fourteen, nearly fifteen (Book III ch. 9): the one wolf named below the usual age of sixteen (§4), an exception the books make because he already lives at the flame. Pilkoji’s foster-son in all but name. After 1430 he wants to relight the flame and cannot, because the Pact cannot be re-sworn by one man. He is the brotherhood’s last wolf.

14. Their fate in Book V [Saga invention]

  • 1430. The flame dies with Vytautas. There will be no more krikštas. The Iron Wolf is seen dissolving into mist on Trakai island (Book V ch. 1). It is not leaving; the house it stood for has lost its last keeper of the fire.
  • 1435, Pabaiskas. The field holds thousands of unburned dead (bestiary.md §24). The last dozen wolves, led by Pilkoji, spend a winter burning them. Most die doing it.
  • 1440. Žygimantas Kęstutaitis, Kęstutis’s last son, is murdered at Trakai. The wolves, too few, fail him. The Iron Wolf’s last howl rises from the lake (Book V ch. 22): the pack of the dead mourning the last of the prince they were first sworn to.
  • Kūčios 1430. Pilkoji gives Kęstutis’s silver wolf (and the cracked pebble), kept from Vytautas’s body since 27 October, to the Flame-Keeper (the “Book V keeper” of power_systems.md §5), as D11 and Book IV have it, not at her death.- The end (Vėlinės, 2 Nov 1449). Pilkoji carries the Iron Wolf’s skull into the oldest bog in the Dykra and lies down beside it. The Flame-Keeper is now the last sworn wolf (he gives the silver wolf to Rimgailė at Palanga in 1450, Book V ch. 29–30). His arc ends when he lets the gods become stories (books/character_arcs.md §4) and, at the series’ close, chooses whether to lie down in the bog or to go home and be named by the fire one last time (§5.5, Leaving). The author decides.
  • The echo. The brotherhood leaves behind half-made wolves: failed candidates and the sons of wolves, throat-marked but never named into a flame. Their descendants are what Olaus Magnus heard about in 1555, Lithuanian and Livonian “werewolves” breaking into cellars on Christmas night for beer. They are the brotherhood dwindled into a drunken folk-tale, the way the giants dwindled into men (bestiary.md §13). The last trace is old Thiess of Jürgensburg in 1692, telling his judges he was one of God’s hounds, and believing it.
  • Epilogue (Book V ch. 30): the grandfather’s “Kai miškai buvo žali, jis buvo” is true of the brotherhood as well as the omen.
